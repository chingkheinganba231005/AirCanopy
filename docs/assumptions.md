# Assumptions

Values the project uses that are not measured or taken from a verified source. Each says where it
is used, why it is acceptable for now, and what will replace it. Results that depend on an
assumption are labelled with its ID.

| ID | Assumption | Used in | Why acceptable for now | Replaced by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A-001 | One storey is 3.0 m tall, when a building's height has to be estimated from its number of storeys | `site/heights.py`; heights labelled `osm:levels` | Storey heights vary (homes are lower than offices); every estimated height is labelled so it can be told apart from a mapped one | Measured heights: city 3D models, Overture Maps heights, or a building-height dataset for the region |
| A-002 | A building with no height has about the median height of the buildings around it: those with a known height whose centres are within 100 m, if there are at least 3 | `site/fill.py`; heights labelled `estimated:neighbour-median` | Neighbouring buildings are often of a similar age and type; the median ignores a single tower among low houses; every filled height is labelled and counted in the site summary, and buildings with too few neighbours stay without a height and out of the grid | Measured heights: Overture Maps, city 3D models, or a building-height dataset |
| A-003 | Kinds of building height are trusted in this order: measured by lidar, mapped (a height in map data), from storeys (A-001), estimated by machine learning, median of neighbours (A-002). Each building keeps the most trusted height available | `site/heights.py` (`HEIGHT_KINDS`), `site/from_overture.py` | Lidar measures heights directly; mapped heights are usually surveyed or taken from plans; storey counts are exact counts with an assumed storey height; machine-learning estimates are known to carry errors of metres; neighbours say nothing about the building itself. The order is a judgement, and every height keeps its kind and dataset, so results can be checked by kind | A comparison against measured heights for sample cities, which can reorder the kinds per region |
| A-004 | An Overture footprint that overlaps a building already in the site by more than half of the smaller of the two is the same building | `site/from_overture.py` (`OVERLAP_SHARE`) | Overture already removes most such doubles; the rule only catches what remains. Real neighbouring buildings share walls, not half their area | A match on both overlap and outline shape |
| A-005 | Between the pixels of the elevation model (about 30 m apart), the ground is a surface of flat triangles on a 20 m grid | `site/terrain.py`, `site/build.py` (`TERRAIN_DX_M`) | The models hold no detail finer than their pixels; any smooth surface through them is a guess, and flat triangles are the simplest one, the same one the view draws | Finer elevation data where it exists (city lidar surveys) |
| A-006 | A building stands on the lowest ground under its footprint | `site/terrain.py` (`lowest_under`) | OpenStreetMap measures a building's height from the lowest point where it meets the ground (Simple 3D Buildings); on a slope, the uphill side of the building is partly below ground, as many hillside buildings are | Building base heights from city 3D models |
| A-007 | There is no tree canopy inside a building's footprint; the canopy map's heights there are set to 0 | `site/canopy.py` (`remove_buildings`) | A tree cannot stand inside a building; where the map shows canopy there, it is the map and the footprints being slightly out of line, or a tree overhanging a low roof | Footprints and canopy from the same images |
| A-008 | Individual trees: the canopy heights are smoothed with a Gaussian of 1 m, and a treetop is the highest point within 2 m around it | `site/canopy.py` (`SMOOTHING_M`, `TOP_RADIUS_M`) | Standard practice for canopy height maps; these values only decide how the canopy is split into crowns, not how much canopy there is | Values tuned against mapped tree registers, where they exist |
| A-009 | The fastest wind expected in a run moves 0.1 lattice cells per time step (Mach number 0.17), which sets the time step | `wind/units.py` (`MAX_LATTICE_SPEED`) | The method's compressibility errors grow with the square of the Mach number (Krüger et al. 2017), while a smaller lattice speed means a shorter time step and more steps for the same simulated time. Step 10 measured the compressibility error in the Taylor–Green vortex: at these speeds it can exceed the grid's own error | Run the validation cases at two speeds and keep the largest whose effect on the answer is acceptable |
| A-010 | Wall model: floors (air above solid) are free-slip, and the first cell of air above a floor feels the shear stress the law of the wall gives for a rough surface, ρ C_d U1², with C_d = (κ / ln(z1/z0))², z1 half a cell, κ = 0.40, applied to the wind of each moment; walls and edges stay no-slip | `wind/walls.py`, `wind/lbm.py` (`stream` with `slip_floors`, `velocity_and_force`) | A city-sized grid cannot resolve the thin layer of air in which the wind slows to zero, so its effect is supplied by the law of the wall, the standard description of the mean wind over a rough surface in neutral air. κ: "the canonical value 0.40" (Andreas et al. 2006, who measured 0.387 ± 0.003 over rough sea ice; laboratory values cluster near 0.37–0.39 and 0.42–0.43), so C_d is uncertain by roughly ±10 % from κ alone. The law holds for the mean wind, and applying it moment by moment is the simplest choice, not the best known. Tested in Step 12: fed from the first cell, the mean wind above it is 10 to 19 % faster than the law of the wall (the "log-layer mismatch"); see A-011 | Test it in an atmospheric boundary layer (P2); then compare with other wall models and with measurements over cities |
| A-011 | Where the air above a floor is deep and open (flat ground, the boundary layer that feeds a site), the wall model of A-010 reads the wind at h_wm = 0.2 δ above the floor instead of the first cell, δ being the depth of the boundary layer, and its stress, ρ C_d(h_wm) \|U\| U, acts on the first cell | `wind/walls.py` (`matched_floor_drag`), `wind/lbm.py` (`velocity_and_force`, `matched`) | The first cells above a wall are the least accurate part of a large-eddy simulation in any code; reading the wind at a height chosen for the flow, with cells small enough there, removes the log-layer mismatch, and "the 'ideal' thickness of the wall-modeled layer is hwm ≈ 0.2δ" (Larsson et al. 2016, Sec. 3.2, after Kawai and Larsson 2012). Tested in Step 12: from 2 cells up to the top, the mean wind is within 3.3 % of the law of the wall. Inside a city the law of the wall may not reach h_wm (street canyons), so there the first cell is still read | Tests on the cube and in a city; a rule for floors among buildings |
| A-012 | The absorbing layer before the outlet: in its last L columns, after each collision, the packets move towards the equilibrium of density 1 and the inlet's velocity (plus g / 2, as post-collision packets carry), f ← f − σ (f − f_target), with σ = strength × (d / L)² at the depth d = 1 … L into the layer; Step 13 uses L = 16 columns and strength 0.2 | `wind/lbm.py` (`absorb`), `wind/solver.py` (`Params.absorb_cells`, `absorb_strength`) | Damping terms of this kind absorb sound before it reaches the boundaries (Xu and Sagaut 2013), and absorbing layers are a common outflow treatment where exact conditions are not accurate enough (Colonius 2004). The share grows gradually into the layer because a sudden onset of damping would itself reflect part of a wave. The target is the undisturbed wind, so a uniform wind passes unchanged (tested). It also pulls a wake back towards the inlet's profile, so the layer must sit well downstream of what is studied. The form, the square profile and the settings are our choice, tested in Step 13 | Characteristic (non-reflecting) outlet conditions; a target taken from the running mean of the flow instead of the inlet's profile; settings tuned on eddies, not only on sound |

## Display-only values

These values change only how a feature is drawn when the data do not give its size. The physics
never reads them.

| Value | Used for | In |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3.0 m per lane (2 lanes if not mapped) | width of a drawn road without a `width` tag | `site/mesh.py`, `DISPLAY_LANE_WIDTH_M` |
| 2.0 m | width of a drawn footway without a `width` tag | `DISPLAY_FOOTWAY_WIDTH_M` |
| 0.15 m | radius of a drawn trunk without a mapped circumference | `DISPLAY_TRUNK_RADIUS_M` |
| 0.6 of the tree height | width of a drawn crown without a mapped `diameter_crown` | `DISPLAY_CROWN_SHARE` |
| 0.6 of the tree height, at most the crown width | depth of a drawn crown | `DISPLAY_CROWN_DEPTH_SHARE` |
| 0.25 of the crown depth | how far a drawn trunk reaches into the crown | `DISPLAY_TRUNK_INTO_CROWN` |
| A sun 40° above the horizon, from the south-east (135° from north), and a clear sky | lighting of the 3D view, chosen so that shadows fall where the starting camera can see them | `ui/src/viewer.ts`, `DISPLAY_SUN_*` and the sky settings |
| 1 km of ground around the site | the surroundings drawn in the 3D view; the physics uses only the site's square | `site/build.py`, `CONTEXT_M` |
| 0.15 m (roads), 0.2 m (footways) | how far above the ground roads and paths are drawn, so the ground does not show through them | `site/mesh.py`, `DISPLAY_ROAD_LIFT_M`, `DISPLAY_FOOTWAY_LIFT_M` |
| 3 m | width of the band that marks the edge of the simulated area | `site/mesh.py`, `DISPLAY_OUTLINE_WIDTH_M` |
| A straight deck from the ground at one end of a bridge to the ground at the other, never below the ground | the drawn height of a road or path on a bridge, which the map does not give | `site/mesh.py`, `_draped` |
